Passing it back

Each dot is a household, ageing left to right from 20 to 90. Its size is its net worth. Rows are wealth deciles for households of the same age, richest at the top. Parents pass ✦ gifts and ✦ inheritances to their adult children at the rates, ages and amounts Australians reported. Tighten the squeeze and watch which rows can still afford to give.

What's real and what's assumed

  • Dot sizes are mean net worth by age and decile. They come from the ABS Household Income and Wealth, Australia 2019-20: each age band's mean and median, and the national percentiles and quintile means. The model reproduces ABS's national P10–P90 exactly, and each age band's mean to within a few percent (≤8%).
  • Gifts and inheritances come from the Productivity Commission's Wealth transfers and their economic effects (2021, HILDA data). That covers how many there are each year, the recipient's age, the average amount at each age, which wealth quintiles receive them, and how much each quintile gets. In dollars the top fifth receives about 5.5× what the bottom fifth does. Relative to what they already have, the bottom fifth gains about 30× more.
  • Who gives: a parent 30 years older than the child (an assumption). The parent's decile is drawn to match the PC's measured link between parents' and children's wealth, which is weak at 20 (0.09) and stronger from 40 (0.49).
  • The squeeze is a what-if, not data. A parent facing extra yearly costs gives only out of the cash left once they're covered. The gift is scaled by 1 − cost ÷ the average cash in the bank for the parent's wealth quintile (ABS: about $9k for the bottom fifth, $233k for the top). Inheritances are left unchanged.
  • One second is one year. The grid is 240 households, so every dot stands for about 40,000 real ones. Every age has the same number of dots.
