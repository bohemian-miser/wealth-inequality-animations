Passing it back

Each dot is a household, ageing left to right from 20 to 90 as the years run from 1970. Rows are income deciles, highest at the top; a dot's size is its wealth. Households rent and save, buy a starter home when they can, trade up, and pay it off. Owners' wealth rises with house prices, and once they own outright they pass ● help to their child (same row, 25 years younger). At 90, their ● estate passes to the child, or to the grandchild if the child already owns outright and the grandchild still rents.

What's real and what's assumed

  • Incomes are estimated from ABS 2019-20 figures (Household Income and Wealth, table 10.1): household disposable income by decile and age, built from ABS's equivalised income percentiles for each age band. ABS hasn't released a newer survey: the 2020-21 and 2021-22 collections were cancelled (COVID-19), and the 2023-24 results were withheld. A household stays in its decile all its life, and so does its family line.
  • House prices start from the ABS mean dwelling value, $685k in 2019-20. That's the value of all dwellings including units, divided by their number; ABS publishes a mean, not a median. Each year's price is that, scaled by the OECD price-to-income ratio, so it is a price at 2019-20 incomes: $376k in 1985, $818k in 2024. The OECD ratio is per person, and households have shrunk, so the early years are somewhat overstated. Prices before 1970 are held at the 1970 level, and after 2025 at the 2025 level, as a what-if, not a forecast. A home is always worth this year's price, so owners' wealth rises with it.
  • Buying: a first home is a starter, at 70% of the mean, bought with a 20% deposit once a 30-year loan's repayments fit the lending limit (a household that can pay cash for the mean dwelling buys that). From 10 years on, it trades up to the mean dwelling as soon as a 20-year loan's repayments fit. Gifts and estates go into savings: they can make a deposit, but a mortgage runs its course (no offset accounts).
  • The mortgage rate is a real 3.3%: the rates actually paid (the RBA's standard variable rate, then its discounted rate from 2004), 8.4% on average from 1970 to 2025, minus each year's inflation. That's one rate for all years. The real rate averaged 1.7% before 1990 and 4.2% since.
  • Calibrated: the saving rate (8% of income, before 65) and the lending limit are fitted so the model's 2019-20 renting, mortgaged and owned-outright shares by age match ABS's. The limit is 18% of income at the real rate. In first-year repayments at the average nominal rate that's about 31% of income, close to the usual bank test. Owners outright give 4.8% of income to their child. That's set so the gifts total the Productivity Commission's figure for gifts from parents, about $1,200 per household a year.
  • Checked, not fitted: estates passed down come to about $9,200 per household a year in the model, about 1.7× the PC's HILDA-based estimate for 2018 ($5,300). It runs high because there is no surviving spouse, aged care or other heirs. The model has too few owners under 35, and its two lowest rows never buy.
  • Assumptions: each child is 25 years younger than their parent, in the same row. Everyone dies at 90 and spends their savings down from 65. There is no super, shares or investment property.
