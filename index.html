Passing it back

How to read this

Each dot is a household, ageing left to right as the years run from 1970. Rows group households by lifetime income, highest at the top; a dot's size is its wealth. Households rent and save, buy when they can, trade up, and pay it off; owners' homes rise in value with prices, and owners pass ● help to their children. When a household dies, its ● estate passes to its child, usually in their 50s or 60s. Each dot in a row is a different household born in a different year, so a row is not one life: older dots bought when houses were cheaper.

What's real and what's assumed

  • Rows are lifetime-income tenths. Incomes come from ABS 2019-20 household disposable incomes by age (Household Income and Wealth), pulled slightly towards each age's middle, and vary within each row (one or two earners, careers); young households earn less until 30. ABS hasn't released a newer survey (2020-21 and 2021-22 were cancelled; 2023-24 was withheld).
  • Each household has its own house price: richer rows buy dearer homes, scaled by ABS dwelling values by income, with a spread for where people live. It has its own age at death (ABS life tables; a couple's household ends at the second death), and its parent is the household 30 years older in the same row.
  • House prices start from the ABS mean dwelling value, $685k in 2019-20, and move with the OECD price-to-income ratio, corrected for shrinking households (Census), so they are prices in 2019-20 terms: $294k in 1970, $400k in 1995, $826k in 2025.
  • Mortgages follow each year's actual rates (RBA rates paid, and CPI): a deposit on a starter home, the bank's test of the first year's repayments, variable-rate repayments, inflation wearing the debt down (fast in the 1970s and 80s), loans repaid by 75. From 10 years on, a household trades up if it can, on a new 20-year loan.
  • Saving, help, estates and separation: saving rises with income. When a child can't yet buy alone, a parent who owns outright may help once (40% of parents do) with 10% of its wealth, capped; about a third of first buyers get help, marked with a gold outline. About 30% of couples split at some point (ABS: about a third of marriages end in divorce): they sell, keep half the equity and rent again. An estate splits between two children; the half that reaches the row comes to about $4,800 per household a year, close to the PC's estimate.
  • Calibrated against history: Census home ownership by age 1971–2021, ABS tenure by age and by income in 2019-20, the rise in 55–64-year-olds with a mortgage since the late 1990s, and first home buyers' average age (32–33, rising to 34–36). The fitted lending limit is about 29% of income and the first-home deposit about 17%.
  • Young households earn less until couples form; the age they reach full income follows ABS's median age at first marriage, which rose from 22 in 1971 to 30 in 2021. Before 1970, prices follow the Macrohistory Database (Stapledon's series), and rates use 1959's regulated rate with each year's inflation, so the 1950s' high inflation wore mortgages away.
  • Where it's off: older households own a little too often, and there is no public housing. Statistics weight households by Australia's real age mix each year. After 2025, prices and rates are held: a what-if, not a forecast.
