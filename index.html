Passing it back

Each dot is a household, ageing left to right as the years run from 1970. Rows group households by lifetime income, highest at the top; a dot's size is its wealth. Households rent and save, buy when they can, trade up, and pay it off; owners' homes rise in value with prices, and owners pass ● help to their children. When a household dies, its ● estate passes to its child, usually in their 50s or 60s. Each dot in a row is a different household born in a different year, so a row is not one life: older dots bought when houses were cheaper.

What's real and what's assumed

  • Rows are lifetime-income tenths. Incomes come from ABS 2019-20 household disposable incomes by age (Household Income and Wealth), pulled towards each age's middle, because one year's income is far more spread out than a lifetime's; the pull is fitted. ABS hasn't released a newer survey (2020-21 and 2021-22 were cancelled; 2023-24 was withheld).
  • Each household has its own house price (where it buys, spread around the ABS mean dwelling value) and its own age at death (spread as in ABS life tables; a couple's household ends at the second death). Its parent is the household 30 years older in the same row.
  • House prices start from the ABS mean dwelling value, $685k in 2019-20, and move with the OECD price-to-income ratio, so they are prices at 2019-20 incomes: $376k in 1985, $818k in 2024. The ratio is per person, so the early years are somewhat overstated.
  • Mortgages follow each year's actual rates (RBA rates paid, and CPI): a 10% deposit on a first home (a starter, below its full price), the bank's test of the first year's repayments, variable-rate repayments, and inflation wearing the debt down, fast in the 1970s and 80s. After 10 years a household trades up if it can.
  • Calibrated against history: Census home ownership by age 1981–2021, ABS tenure by age and by income in 2019-20, and the rise in 55–64-year-olds still paying a mortgage since the late 1990s. Saving (out of income above a living-cost floor), the lending limit (25% of income in first-year repayments) and the two spreads are fitted; gifts are set so their total matches the Productivity Commission's figure for gifts from parents.
  • Where it's off: first buyers are younger than ABS's average (32–34), too few high-income households rent, estates passed down run about twice the PC's estimate, and every household keeps one lifetime-income row and one child. After 2025, prices and rates are held: a what-if, not a forecast.
