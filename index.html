Passing it back

Each dot is a household, ageing left to right from 20 to 90. Rows are income deciles, highest at the top; a dot's size is its wealth. Every household rents and saves, buys the house when it can, pays it off, and then passes ● help to its child in the same row, 25 years younger. Move the house price and watch who gets to own outright, and when.

What's real and what's assumed

  • Incomes are ABS household disposable incomes by decile and age, 2019-20 (Household Income and Wealth, table 10.1). A household stays in its decile all its life.
  • The house is the ABS mean dwelling price, $685k in 2019-20 (ABS publishes a mean, not a median). The year marks show what a house would cost at 2019-20 incomes, using that year's price-to-income ratio (OECD). In 1985 it would be about $376k; by 2024 it's $818k.
  • The mortgage rate is a real rate of 3.6%: the RBA's standard variable rate from 1970 to 2025 (8.8% on average) minus inflation. The model works in 2019-20 dollars, so the price slider is the only thing that changes.
  • The rules: save 10% of income, buy once the loan can be repaid within 30 years on 17% of income, and put that 17% towards it until it's paid off. After that, give 4.75% of income to the child until the child owns outright too. These numbers are calibrated, not guessed. The saving and housing shares are fitted to ABS home ownership by age, with each age group run at the price it faced around 30. The giving share is set so the gifts total matches the Productivity Commission's figure for gifts from parents: about $1,200 per household a year. The model under-predicts how many under-45s own, and has 45–64s paying off too soon. Real buyers start cheaper and trade up.
  • Assumptions: a child is 25 years younger than their parent and in the same row. Every generation lives the same life at the price shown. There are no inheritances, super or investments. Wealth here is housing equity plus savings.
